After working at several consultancies between 2011 and 2017, one contradiction kept jumping out at me: professional services firms sell people's time, yet almost all of them have remarkably clunky systems for knowing who is actually available.

## The Spreadsheet and Gantt Gap

At Huron Consulting and Berkeley Research Group, teams burned hours every week managing capacity manually—usually through sprawling Excel sheets where consultants self-reported their bandwidth.

At Huron, we used a resource management platform called Retain. Staff projected hours onto long-term engagements, producing large Gantt charts showing pockets of capacity two to six months out. Useful for macro planning, but too rigid for day-to-day reality.

At Berkeley, the problem was the opposite: sudden, urgent requests from law firms and healthcare clients meant consultants swung abruptly between 14-hour days and sitting on the bench. To smooth out those spikes, I built a small internal desktop tool where consultants could raise their hand and broadcast short-term availability along with their skills. Over time, it evolved into a quiet two-way marketplace between analysts looking for work and managers looking for help.

## From Consulting Back to B2B SaaS

When I left Berkeley in late 2016 to consult independently, I had no immediate plan to commercialize that idea. I spent the year building healthcare analytics solutions for clients while tinkering with [WorkDive](/development/productivity/2016/12/16/workdive.html), an SMS time-tracker I had built earlier.

WorkDive taught me an expensive lesson: selling a B2C productivity tool in a crowded market is brutal. I kept using it myself to log my own hours, and started looking for a B2B problem where I already understood the buyers.

Resource management for consultancies was staring right at me. I knew the workflow, I had already built a prototype people loved, and most of my professional network lived in the exact firms experiencing the pain.

Early conversations with consulting friends were encouraging—everyone agreed staffing was messy—though I immediately sensed subtle pushback underneath the enthusiasm. I put pen to paper anyway, did a quick market scan, and made the first GitHub commit for Staff Mapper in July 2017.

I had no idea I would still be building it years later.
