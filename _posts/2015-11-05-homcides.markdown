After talking with my friend Lesley O'Neill at UGA Law School—who spent a summer working alongside inmates on death row—I found myself curious about a basic empirical question: does historical U.S. data actually suggest that capital punishment deters violent crime?

Lesley described the tangled realities behind nearly every case she encountered: absent parents, mental illness, instability, and violent tragedy. Rather than debating the question abstractly, I pulled together historical figures on violent crimes, death sentences, and executions from **1930 to 2012** (where national series were not reported in earlier decades, values are plotted as zero).

## Reading the Historical Pattern

Violent crime rose steadily from 1960 until peaking in 1992, then declined sharply. Death-row populations leveled off shortly afterward, while annual executions climbed from 31 in 1992 to 98 in 1999 before falling again.

Notice also the flat zero line between 1968 and 1976: the Supreme Court suspended capital punishment in 1972—not as unconstitutional in principle, but because its application was arbitrary and unreliable under the Eighth Amendment. As Lesley noted, states rewrote their statutes by 1976 and the Court reinstated it, though whether arbitrariness was truly resolved remains deeply contested.

## Why Geography Confounds Deterrence

Even if you wanted to isolate a causal link between executions and declining violent crime, national aggregates hide a striking geographic skew. Capital punishment is far less about the "worst of the worst" than about **which county prosecutes the case**:

- Mass-casualty defendants in states like Colorado have received life sentences, while single-victim crimes in Georgia or Louisiana regularly draw death sentences.
- Just **2% of U.S. counties account for 56% of all inmates on death row**. Between 2010 and 2014, Caddo Parish, Louisiana sentenced more people to death per capita than any other county in the country.

A deeper causal analysis would need state-level panels controlling for population growth, legal status by jurisdiction, and crime category. Even at the macro level, though, the data makes one thing clear: any straightforward deterrence narrative runs straight into how unevenly the penalty is applied.
